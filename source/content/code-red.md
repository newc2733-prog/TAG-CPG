## Background

Exsanguinating haemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable death after major trauma. Advances in the understanding of trauma pathophysiology and transfusion science have led to significant improvements in survival from bleeding in recent years. This CPG describes the key elements required for successful resuscitation in critically injured and actively bleeding trauma patients, typically known at Royal London Hospital as ‘Code Red’.

> **Note:** This CPG is intended for the most profoundly critically-ill and unstable Code Red patient. All recommendations, doses and manoeuvres may be titrated or adjusted as required, either initially or as resuscitation progresses and physiological stability and reserve improve. In this context, ‘’active bleeding’’ refers to **severe haemorrhage** at a rate causing shock and minimal or no response to volume resuscitation.

## Anaesthetic Considerations

Keep all elements of anaesthesia as simple and standardised as possible

### Induction for common Code Red Scenarios:

| Scenario | Induction |
|---|---|
| **Awake** | Fentanyl: Ketamine: Rocuronium 1mcg/kg : 1mg/kg : 1mg/kg |
| **Shocked & Drowsy** | Ketamine: Rocuronium 1mg/kg : 1mg/kg |
| **Unconscious or Peri-arrest** | Rocuronium 100mg |

- The **3:2:1** ratio should be **reserved** for haemodynamically **INTACT** patients

### Maintenance:

- If intubated in the field, top up paralysis on patient’s arrival with **Rocuronium** and note the time.
- Administer **Co-amoxiclav 1.2g** early (unless known allergy).
- Maintain analgesia and anaesthesia with **intermittent boluses of Fentanyl 25-50mcg, increasing as tolerated.** Beware of large doses of Fentanyl – it can cause significant hypotension in bleeding trauma patients.
- In early profound hypovolaemia before surgical control, maintenance may be achieved with **aliquots of Ketamine (20 - 40mg).**
- In patients that are approaching haemodynamic stability, undergoing transfer or awaiting an ACCU bed, anaesthesia may be maintained with **low dose propofol infusion (2-15 ml/hour).**
- In the operating theatre utilize cautious, small aliquots of fentanyl or ketamine as tolerated until the resuscitation is well established. Once physiology permits, a volatile can be added. Typically, **Sevoflurane at 0.3 - 0.5 MAC.**
- **TIVA should not be used in Code Red/bleeding advanced trauma patients**

### Context & Rationale:

**Haemodynamic status** - Drugs take longer to act due to prolonged circulation times and volumes of distribution. Patients are intolerant to standard drug regimens due to catecholamine depletion and/or myocardial depression.

**Rocuronium** - Usually given as a 100mg push. Even in morbidly obese patients where IBW dosing is typical.

**Nature of injuries** - The principle behind a fentanyl-heavy approach in trauma patients is to restrict the neuroendocrine stress response to tissue injury without blunting the protective autonomic responses to blood loss, and opening the microvascular capacitance slowly. Therefore, a polytrauma patient with a high tissue injury burden will require (and tolerate) more fentanyl earlier in their timeline than a patient exsanguinating from a single stab wound.

**Peri-arrest patients** - Consider zero PEEP and low tidal volumes initially as a sudden rise in intrathoracic pressure with IPPV may adversely affect preload in the volume depleted patient. As soon as the patient is volume restored, return PEEP to the ventilation strategy.

## Resuscitation Considerations

| | |
|---|---|
| **Permissive hypotension** | Aim to maintain SBP ≥ 60-70mmHg (see below) |
| **Blood product ratio 1:1:1** | **Avoid crystalloids** |
| **ROTEM Guidance** | Maintain pack A/B ratios until ROTEM results available, then products as per <a class="xref" href="#rotem-algorithm.s2">algorithm</a> **in addition** to packs |
| **Tranexamic Acid** | **2g** (within first 3 hours)<br>**1g** (every whole body transfusion) |
| **Calcium** | **10 ml 10% Calcium Chloride**<br>Keep ionised Ca<sup>2+</sup> > 1.0 |
| **Bicarbonate** | **1ml/kg of 8.4%** for severe, profound acidosis |
| **Potassium** | **10 ml 10% Calcium Chloride** (omit if Ca<sup>2+</sup> > 1.3)<br>**Insulin/ Dextrose:** 50u insulin in 50ml of 50% dextrose. |
| **Noradrenaline/Adrenaline** | Should only be considered once the haemorrhage has been controlled and patient’s volume restored. |

### Context & Rationale:

The goal is to **maintain organ perfusion and minimise blood loss** until definitive haemostasis can be achieved.

Definitive bleeding control may be via surgery or Interventional Radiology. In general, critically unstable patients should be brought to theatre. TAG Consultant assessment of the physiology should inform this decision in discussion with the Trauma Surgeon and Trauma Team Leader.

### Volume Replacement:

- In active bleeding, **permissive hypotension** is accepted. This should be kept to the minimum duration necessary. In most patients a **SBP of ±60mmHg** can be accepted. In the elderly or those with known or suspected stenotic coronary or aortic lesions, aim for a **SBP of ±70mmHg**. In patients with confirmed traumatic brain injury or spinal cord injury a risk-benefit decision should be made based on the severity of bleeding and likely degree of compromise to the cerebral/spinal cord perfusion pressure.
- **Maintain a ratio of PRBC:FFP:Platelets of 1:1:1**. Blood packs come in the following format:
    - **Pack A:** 6 PRBC (O negative/positive blood from local fridge) + 6 FFP
    - **Pack B:** 6 PRBC (from fridge as above) + 6 FFP + 2 Cryo + 1 Platelets (1 bag = 6 individual units)
    - **Keep ahead of requirements:** Blood bank will thaw 6 units FFP at a time as standard during a Code Red but will defrost 2 Pack B on request.
- Resuscitate according to the **lactate and base deficit** which should improve linearly with bleeding control and volume restoration.
- Refer to Vascular Access CPG and RATS Handbook for Vascular access and Belmont procedures.

> When haemostasis is achieved, the strategy should change to one of aggressive **restoration of physiological normality including MAP ≥ 65mmHg.**

### Coagulopathy & Metabolic State:

- Give blood products as above until haemostasis and adequate blood pressure. Aim to provide targeted coagulation therapy via **ROTEM guidance.**
- **No crystalloids** should be administered during the resuscitation phase.
- Code Red patients should receive **2g of Tranexamic Acid** prior to arrival in theatre or within the first 3 hours of injury. Further doses (1g) should be given if fibrinolysis is apparent on ROTEM or every whole-body transfusion (12 PRBC + 2x Pack B).
- **Hypocalcaemia** results from chelation by citrate in the blood products and is common in all major resuscitations. Keep the **ionised calcium > 1.0** with regular boluses of **Calcium Chloride** (**5 - 10ml of 10% solution**). Beware Calcium Gluconate: dosing requires 3x more than Calcium Chloride per unit volume.
- The pH should improve with resuscitation, but prolonged acidosis impairs myocardial function and promotes arrhythmias, vasodilation and coagulopathy. If **pH < 7** consider **1ml/kg of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate.**
- Vasoplegia should be treated with **noradrenaline**. This is in the context of indicators that haemorrhage is controlled and volume status is approaching normal, but blood pressure remains poor. Consider early echocardiography to evaluate quality of ventricular contractility and early ECMO consultation.

### Potassium:

- Big volumes of PRBCs and large burden of tissue injury or prolonged hypoxaemia will result in a significant burden of free potassium ions made worse by acidosis. Renal hypoperfusion may additionally prevent adequate clearance. The majority of patients that die on the operating table do so from uncontrolled hyperkalaemia. **It cannot be overstated how important it is to anticipate and manage this as a complication.**
- Management strategy should include the following:
    - **10 ml 10% Calcium Chloride** (omit if ionised Calcium >1.3)
    - **Insulin/Dextrose:** 50u insulin in 50ml, 50% dextrose, infuse at 2 - 5ml/hour. Maximum infusion rate is 200ml/hour (will administer over 15 min).
    - Effects of insulin will be prolonged post resuscitation therefore **hypoglycaemia must be sought and treated aggressively** for the first 24 – 48 hours.
    - **Consider 1ml/kg 8.4% Sodium Bicarbonate** (if K<sup>+</sup> > 6)
    - **Avoid initiating filtration in theatre: clearance is inadequate, logistic burden is high.**

## Practical & Non-Technical Considerations

### Arterial lines:

- Should not delay other lifesaving therapies or procedures. May be deferred until sufficient team members are available and initial resuscitation priorities are met.
- Palpation of a carotid pulse, monitoring ETCO<sub>2</sub> or visualization of the heart through the open thorax are sufficient indicators of a beating heart and perfusing rhythm.
- Venous blood gas from the trauma line can be used to determine calcium, potassium, lactate and base deficit.

### Temperature Management:

- All efforts should be made to achieve patient **core temperature > 36.5<sup>O</sup>C.**
- Set the ambient **theatre temperature to 25<sup>O</sup>C.**
- Keep the patient **dry and covered** as much as possible with a **Bair Hugger** and blankets.
- The patient should be on an **Inditherm** mattress.
- All fluids should go through a fluid warmer.

### Post Operatively:

- If acidosis and temperature control are challenging after surgery is complete, consider remaining in theatre for continued **warming** and **resuscitation.**
- Maintain patient in theatre until blood gas, temperature and ongoing transfusion requirements are satisfactory for management on ACCU by a bedside nurse.
- Handover should be to **ACCU Consultant** with clear outline of prior events, progress of resuscitation and anticipated problems or ongoing requirements.

## Traumatic Cardiac Arrest / Peri-Arrest Algorithm

![Traumatic cardiac arrest / peri-arrest algorithm (European Resuscitation Council Guidelines 2021)](fig/code-red-tca.jpg)

- Trauma Patient in Arrest/ Peri-Arrest → Non-traumatic arrest likely? **YES** → ALS
- **NO** → START CPR. Address reversible causes simultaneously (Hypoxaemia, Hypovolaemia, Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade):
    1. Control external catastrophic haemorrhage
    2. Secure airway and maximise oxygenation
    3. Bilateral chest decompression (thoracostomies)
    4. Relieve tamponade (penetrating chest injury)
    5. Proximal vascular control (REBOA/manual aortic compression)
    6. Pelvic splint
    7. Blood products / Massive Haemorrhage Protocol
- Expertise? Equipment? Environment? Elapsed time since loss of vital signs < 15 min? → Resuscitative Thoracotomy
- **ROSC YES** → Pre-hospital: immediate transport to appropriate hospital. In-hospital: damage control surgery / resuscitation
- **ROSC NO** → Consider termination of resuscitation
