## Decision-making Principles

- For the purposes of the immediate management of the airway, the distinction between blunt or penetrating trauma is less important.
- Actual or impending airway compromise, other injuries or clinical course may preclude multidisciplinary input (primarily Trauma or, in selected cases, Airway Surgeons).
- Small, seemingly superficial injuries can cause significant occult injuries.
- Injuries of concern include the following:
    - **Vascular injuries**: May cause compression and distortion, bleeding control may be difficult, airway management may be difficult.
    - **Airway injury**: May cause airway disruption, massive surgical emphysema, obstruction and ventilatory failure.
    - **Zone 1 injury**: May penetrate the thorax causing subclavian and brachiocephalic injury, lung injury and tension pneumothorax.

### There are 2 questions that guide initial management strategy:

**1. Is immediate/urgent intubation indicated?**

Injuries that do not require immediate management allow time to gather a comprehensive clinical picture ± imaging, before a bespoke plan can be made with appropriate specialist input. In reality, many patients with neck injuries require immediate or urgent intervention.

**2. Is there a suspected injury to the airway?**

- Information may be limited, imaging may be unavailable, and external injuries may be misleading, particularly with penetrating injuries which can cause occult damage distant from the puncture site.
- Airway injuries are more common in penetrating trauma.
- Blunt trauma may cause serious injuries including laryngeal fractures, tracheal tears, and laryngotracheal separation.
- Injury mechanism should be correlated with clinical findings to identify potential airway injuries.
- Signs suggestive of laryngotracheal injury:
    - Abrasions, bruising and deformity overlying airway structures
    - Stridor/ hoarseness/drooling
    - Haemoptysis
    - Surgical emphysema/bubbles at wound site
    - Ventilatory failure

## Urgent intubation NOT required

When immediate or urgent intubation is not required, imaging can be performed to characterise the nature of the injury and multidisciplinary expertise can be consulted to create an individualised plan. Advanced techniques such as AFOI, rigid bronchoscopy, awake tracheostomy, use of double lumen tubes and standby VV ECMO can be considered.

## Urgent intubation required; NO airway injury suspected

**Plan A – Emergency Department RSI**<br>**Plan B – FONA**

- Refer: Emergency Department RSI CPG
- Be aware that external compression of structures may distort the airway
- This may be appropriate in patients with:
    - **Vascular injuries** where airway management is being expedited
    - **Zone 3 neck injuries** with concurrent max-fax injuries. Although external anatomy to the face and oral cavity may be grossly distorted, direct laryngoscopy is usually possible following administration of NMBs. The patient is usually best managed in a position of comfort (e.g., sitting up) until induction, with **2x suction** available to manage pooled secretions and blood.
- **LMAs are usually not suitable for these patients**

## Urgent intubation required; airway injury IS suspected

### Plan A – Ambuscope assisted DL/VL

- **Prepare the neck** with local anaesthetic & adrenaline before commencement.
- Involves an RSI with direct (DL) or videolaryngoscopy (VL) and a second operator with an ambuscope. DL/VL allows evaluation of the hypopharynx and larynx. The ambuscope is advanced to and through the laryngeal inlet under vision. The inside of the trachea may be inspected, and the injury identified and characterised. Thereafter an ETT can be advanced beyond the injury (bevel orientated to face the injury to minimise the risk of creating a false passage or extending the injury).
- Use a small ETT (size 6.0 - 7.0).
- **Positive pressure ventilation (PPV) should be avoided** as it may cause massive surgical emphysema. **HFNO** may be useful at low flows (20 - 40L/min).
- Standard DAS difficult airway guidelines are rarely appropriate in this situation where PPV and surgical cricothyroidotomy may be unsuccessful or worsen the injury.
- In preparation for airway soiling have the following prepared:
    - **2x Suction** – 1 connected to ambuscope; 1 connected to yankauer
    - **2x 20ml syringe** each containing **dilute adrenaline** (10mcg/ml) + **Tranexamic Acid** 500mg for injecting down the ambuscope.
- **Failure of this technique should be followed by immediate tracheostomy**

### Plan B – Surgical tracheostomy

- Prepare equipment and personnel prior to any attempts at intubation.
- FONA via the cricothyroid membrane is often inappropriate or impossible due to distorted anatomy. Advancing bougies or an ETT blindly may lead to the creation of a false passage.
- Where time, equipment and expertise allow, **awake surgical tracheostomy** may be a preferential Plan A.
