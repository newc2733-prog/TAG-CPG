> **AN INFLATED REBOA BALLOON IS A TIME CRITICAL, LIFE THREATENING EMERGENCY - EVEN ONCE HAEMORRHAGE CONTROLLED. AIM TOTAL BALLOON TIME < 30min.**

## Definition

Non-compressible torso haemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable trauma deaths. Pelvic fractures and torso vascular injuries are two important sources of this bleeding and contribute to one third of all trauma deaths at the Royal London Hospital. Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta (REBOA) involves placement of an endovascular balloon in the proximal or distal aorta and is used to control catastrophic haemorrhage and improve afterload in traumatic cardiac arrest and haemorrhagic shock states to facilitate transfer of a patient to place of definitive control, ie theatre or interventional radiology. A specific ‘CODE RED REBOA’ trauma call may be put out, however, any CODE RED may be upgraded upon arrival of the patient to Resus.

## Clinical use

A REBOA sheath +/- catheter can be inserted pre-hospital or in ED Resus, dictated by senior decision makers present (Trauma Team Leader, TAG, Consultant, Trauma Surgeon). It provides temporary control and balloon time should be minimised. Patients will often be moved straight to theatre on HEMS monitoring.

Inflation time is a **crucial** detail. REBOA is associated with numerous risks and complications including vascular, renal, splanchnic and spinal cord ischaemia - the risk increases exponentially with balloon inflation time.

The REBOA trolley is located in Bay 8 of Resus - it contains transducer equipment including the ‘splitter cable’ to allow 2 waveforms to be transduced (REBOA sheath and catheter).

Default setup is double transducer - **R**ed is to the **R**EBOA tip and is the upper of the two traces (scale 0-300mmHg), **B**lue **B**elow this and represents aortic pressure distal to the balloon (scale 0-100mmHg).

Zone 1 REBOA – primarily for abdominal injuries and cardiac arrest, occlusion above the coeliac trunk and below the left subclavian artery. Expect the physiological changes to be profound.

Zone 3 REBOA – primarily for pelvic injuries, occlusion above the bifurcation of the iliacs and below the renal arteries.

## REBOA variations

1. The Common Femoral Artery (CFA) has been cannulated with a 4Fr arterial line. This can be used for Invasive Blood Pressure (IBP) and if needed exchanged for a size 8Fr arterial sheath through which REBOA can be placed.
2. The CFA has been cannulated with a size 8Fr arterial sheath. Again, this can be used for IBP monitoring.
3. The REBOA catheter has been inserted through the 8Fr sheath. This means there will be two lumens to transduce. The side port on the 8Fr arterial sheath and the arterial line lumen on the REBOA catheter.
4. Partial REBOA (P-REBOA) - the REBOA balloon has been partially deflated to reduce distal ischaemia and reperfusion injury. Arterial pressure from the side arm of the sheath (downstream of the balloon) will not usually read zero, but a relatively low number this is known as a ‘baseline’ occlusion pressure. The aim is to deflate the REBOA balloon to allow enough flow through to raise this ‘baseline’ mean by 5-10mmHg or the return of a small pulsatile wave form, whichever comes first.
5. Complete REBOA (C-REBOA) the REBOA balloon is inflated to occlude the aorta. This is balanced against the risk of sustained complete aortic occlusion, primarily distal ischaemia and associated reperfusion injury. **The period of C-REBOA should be less than 20minutes.**

## Tips and tricks

- Remember ‘hard to transduce’ when zeroing - the ‘stiff tube’ of the transducer connects to the patient and the ‘soft tube’ side of the transducer goes to the fluid bag. If you turn the tap to face the hard side (‘hard to transduce’) then you are open to air and able to zero the transducer.
- HEMS will attach transducers to the patients leg - be aware the cables will be stretched between monitor and patient
- Both traces / waveforms are arterial. However, for clarity the ‘CVP’ wave colour is used for the 4Fr arterial line / 8Fr sheath (**B**lue **B**elow).
- The lower trace scale is set to 100mmHg rather than the ‘normal’ scale of 0-30mmHg because if the pressure is greater than 30mmHg you will not see the trace on the monitor
- **Do NOT confuse the arterial line lumen and the balloon lumen on the REBOA catheter** - unintentional inflation / deflation of the balloon may have catastrophic results.
- Samples for ABG can be taken from the 4Fr arterial line, 8Fr arterial sheath and REBOA catheter (preferentially the REBOA catheter) - ensure to account for length of catheter when aspirating / flushing. Note, samples may also differ depending where they are taken from.
- Be alert for the potential for error when transducing two waveforms in a high-pressure, multi-personnel environment when there are a number of opportunities for the cables to become confused, this may be where the cables connect to the transducers or at the ‘splitter’ - attach one cable at a time and confirm expected waveform appears before connecting the second
- The transducer set up can be tested for dynamic response by pulling and quickly releasing the ‘Snap-Tab’ on the transducer - this should give you a ‘square’ waveform or a flattened trace.

## Potential issues following balloon deflation

- **Hypotension** - ?Re-inflate balloon, blood products, caution with vasopressors, ongoing resuscitation, (try to optimise circulating volume *before deflation).*
- **Re-perfusion** - Hypothermia, hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia, coagulopathy and cardiac arrest can all occur - consider empirical initiation of insulin infusion (50IU in 50mL 50% Dextrose), sodium bicarbonate (1mL/kg 8.4%) and calcium chloride (10mL 10%). Note these effects may be worst 30-60min post-deflation and beyond, not necessarily immediately following deflation. Frequent ABGs / ROTEMs are required to monitor.
