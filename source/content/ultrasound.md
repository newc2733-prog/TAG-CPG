## Introduction

It has been demonstrated that focused ultrasonography by acute care clinicians can achieve high levels of sensitivity and specificity. However, it is explicit that ultrasound use is an adjunct which augments clinical examination, neither replacing it nor ignoring the cumulative body of other clinical evidence (or lack thereof) for a diagnosis. It should be used to answer specific, focused, and binary questions generated by that examination. POCUS has been shown to lead to a change in polytrauma management decisions or triage in as many as 30% of cases and improves diagnostic accuracy by 70-90%.

This Clinical Practice Guideline describes the London’s Air Ambulance focussed protocol for consideration for use by Trauma Anaesthetists with relevant skills. This guideline does not replace formal ultrasound training or accreditation with relevant professional bodies.

## Key Principles

- The POCUS protocol should be used by clinicians to answer specific diagnostic or therapeutic clinical questions taken in context of the mechanism of injury, the physiological parameters and the clinical findings on examination. Where there is difficulty in differentiating causes of shock or in the completely undifferentiated patient, the full protocol should be deployed unless there is reasonable justification to exclude an element.
- Clinicians should not become task-fixated or delay resuscitation to conduct lengthy POCUS examinations.
- If it is not possible to achieve a satisfactory image within a reasonable time frame, or if the image is not consistent with the clinical picture, **then trust the clinical picture.**

## Anaesthesia Key Principles

The following scenarios are envisaged for the use of POCUS for Trauma:

- **Critically injured trauma patients with a specific diagnostic or pathophysiological question answerable through POCUS.**
- **Cardiac arrest.**
- **Vascular access.**

> **LAA POCUS Protocol:** ‘Pump – Pleura – Pouring Blood?’

### 1. ‘Pump’

Ultrasound is most sensitive and specific for the evaluation of patients with precordial or transthoracic wounds (sensitivity 93%, specificity 99% for cardiac tamponade). Therefore, a sub-xiphoid view of the heart should be achieved intending to answer the following questions:

- **Is there a pericardial collection?**
- **Is the left ventricle empty or full?**
- **Is the left ventricle contracting well or poorly?**
- **Is the right ventricle contracting well or poorly?**

This single window allows an opportunity to distinguish between obstructive, hypovolaemic and cardiogenic shock pictures or to identify when more than one discrete aetiology is present.

### 2. ‘Pleura’

Ultrasound has a higher sensitivity in diagnosing a pneumothorax than clinical examination or chest radiography (positive predictive values 80-90%; negative predictive values 70-90%).

Clinicians should perform a two-point examination bilaterally in the most anterior portion of the chest (dependent on patient position) evaluating for presence or absence of lung sliding to answer the question:

- **Is there a pneumothorax?**

### 3. ‘Pouring Blood?’

Two specific examinations are valuable to identify haemorrhage. A bilateral evaluation in the most basal/ dependent areas of the chest (dependent on patient position) evaluating for presence or absence of a large fluid collection; and the right upper quadrant (RUQ) view of a Trauma FAST Scan evaluating for a fluid collection in Morrison’s pouch or around the liver; in order to answer the following questions:

- **Is there the presence of a massive haemothorax?**
- **Is there the presence of a massive haemoperitoneum?**

Blood is most often found on a Trauma FAST Scan in the RUQ area in patients with multiple intraperitoneal injuries or isolated injury to the liver, spleen, or retroperitoneum. Of the three areas visible in this view (hepato-diaphragmatic, Morrison’s pouch, caudal liver edge and superior para-colic gutter) the caudal liver edge is the area most sensitive for the detection of blood.

Data demonstrates a sensitivity of approximately 70% (range 60-80%) for detection of blood with a specificity of approximately 98-99%. Sensitivity is increased if the patients are hypotensive and have suffered blunt abdominal trauma and approaches 100% in this context.

False-negative results do occur, and this is most frequently in scans performed early in the disease process and interestingly in patients with higher ISS. Therefore, when RUQ scan is negative it is recommended to repeat the examination serially (15 min intervals) where practical as this may reduce error rate by 50%. Thus, in hypotensive patients with a negative study, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for significant abdominal bleeding. It should be noted ultrasound is also poor at detecting injuries to a hollow viscus.

### 4. Cardiac Arrest

As many as 75% of patients in PEA cardiac arrest (medical) are demonstrated to have cardiac activity with ultrasound (pseudo-PEA). Pseudo-PEA has a high correlation with survival compared to true PEA if identified early and treated appropriately. In medical cardiac arrest 94% of patients with cardiac standstill demonstrable on POCUS will not survive to hospital (97.5% positive predictive value for death). After 10 minutes of resuscitation if cardiac standstill remains, there are no survivors, and this benchmark has been recommended as a marker for cessation of resuscitation attempts unless there exist mitigating factors (hypothermia; drug intoxication). In traumatic cardiac arrest Trauma FAST Scan is also a predictor of futility. The absence of pericardial blood or cardiac activity at scan (even with thoracotomy) for blunt or penetrating arrest has been shown to be uniformly fatal.

POCUS may have a role in cardiac arrest and aims to answer questions which may guide resuscitation decisions specifically with respect to the following:

- **Is there a pericardial effusion?** (Consider thoracotomy)
- **Is there a massive haemoperitoneum?** (Early surgery)
- **Is there cardiac standstill?** (Consider futility)

Post ROSC:

- **Is the heart empty or full?** (Hypovolaemia)
- **Is the heart beating well or poorly?** (Cardiogenic shock)

## Limitations

POCUS is used by non-radiologists to answer simple yes/ no questions and to guide treatment decisions. Sensitivity for isolated solid organ injuries can be low and small quantities of blood early in the post-injury phase may be missed. Traumatic aortic pathology cannot be detected by chest or abdominal ultrasound. False negatives will occur; therefore, negative findings should not indicate a final exclusion of diagnoses.
